# How New Grads Should Write a Resume: Landing Interviews Without Work Experience

> This is the third post in the examples series. The first two covered how hiring pipelines work and how applications get filtered. This one gets practical: how do you actually write a new grad resume?

## 1. Course Introduction

The first two posts laid a lot of groundwork. You now know who reads your resume (Recruiters, Hiring Managers, and different readers in different scenarios). You also understand what ATS really is. It's a database, not a judge, and the people actually filtering you are humans.

Now we finally get to the hands-on part: how do you write a resume that lands interviews?

This post is specifically for new grads. If you haven't graduated yet or recently graduated without full-time work experience, this content is for you.

New grads face one core dilemma: you don't have work experience, but the resume is supposed to show what you can do. So what do you do?

Before we start, one important note: this is a Chinese tutorial, but we're teaching you how to write an **English resume for the North American job market**. So every resume description, bullet point, and Summary example below will be in English. That's what you actually put on your resume.

---

## 2. Learning Objectives

Let's recap what the first post covered. As a new grad, you're mainly looking at two scenarios.

The first is large company campus recruiting. Google, Meta, Amazon, and similar companies have dedicated University Recruiting teams who run Career Fairs, Info Sessions, collect resumes in bulk, screen in bulk, interview in bulk, and send offers in bulk. In this model, your resume sits in a pile with thousands of others and goes through standardized screening. Hard metrics carry a lot of weight: school, GPA, major, keyword match.

The second is direct applications to small and mid-size companies. Startups and mid-size tech companies don't have a dedicated campus recruiting pipeline. A Hiring Manager needs someone, posts a JD, and resumes start pouring in. The problem is your resume sits in the same pool as candidates with 2 to 3 years of experience. A lot of new grads don't realize this: you aren't competing with other new grads, you're competing with seasoned professionals.

These two scenarios call for slightly different resumes, but the core question is the same: with no work experience, how do you make someone feel you're worth interviewing?

After this post, you'll know what structure a new grad resume should have, whether to write a Summary, how to use projects to show "what I actually did," and what the most common mistakes are.

## 3. One Key Realization: You Probably Need More Than One Resume

Before we get into structure, I want to call out something a lot of new grads miss.

You probably need more than one resume.

Why? Because even when the job title is identical, the actual work can be completely different.

Take "AI Developer." Different companies recruiting an AI Developer can mean completely different things. Some want someone closer to SDE, writing production code and doing systems integration. Some want someone closer to AI/ML, training and tuning models. Some want someone closer to Data, building pipelines and processing data.

If you send the same resume for all three, none of them feel focused enough.

Going further, if your skills let you apply to multiple Job Families (say Software Engineer, Data Engineer, and Machine Learning Engineer), you should prepare multiple versions of your resume, each tuned for one direction.

What does that mean in practice? Your Summary is different, the skills you highlight are different, the projects you emphasize are different, and even the wording of your Bullet Points is different.

Later examples will dig into how to do this Tailoring (see the 1+N resume method in [05-resume-matrix](../05-resume-matrix/README.md), and how [09-write-bullets](../09-write-bullets/README.md) plus [10-write-summary](../10-write-summary/README.md) write bullets and Summary for each direction). For now, just remember the core point: the days of one resume for every application are over.

## 4. The Core Structure of a New Grad Resume

Let's start with what sections a new grad resume should include and the order they should appear in.

For new grads, the recommended order is:

1. Contact Information
2. Summary. If you have multiple directions, put this at the very top.
3. Education
4. Skills
5. Projects
6. Work Experience (internships, if you have any)

Pay attention to the order. If you have a clear job target and need a Summary to position yourself, the Summary belongs at the very top, even above Education. It's the first line telling the reader "who I am, what I can do."

Let's go through each one.

---

## 5. Contact Information

This part looks the simplest, but plenty of people manage to mess it up.

Include your name, email, phone number, and city. If you have a LinkedIn and GitHub, you can add them. The catch is they need to look respectable.

On GitHub, this is an easy place to lose points. Never link to an empty GitHub. If your GitHub has a few empty repositories or the last commit was two years ago, leave it off. A dead Heroku deployment or a half-finished project only subtracts points. Either clean it up first, or leave it out.

One more thing a lot of people overlook: keep everything visually consistent. URLs that don't align, inconsistent line spacing, a LinkedIn link awkwardly wrapped onto its own line when it could fit on the previous one. These small details make someone who claims to be "detail oriented" look like the opposite.

## 6. Summary: When You Need One, and How to Write It

This question comes up a lot: should a new grad resume open with a Summary?

My advice: **if you have a clear job target and need to tell the reader "who I am," write one. If you don't plan to customize the Summary per application, skip it.**

A bland Summary just wastes valuable resume space. Like this:

> *Passionate developer seeking challenging opportunities to grow and contribute to innovative teams.*

This sentence carries zero information. Anyone can write it. It tells the reader nothing concrete about you.

But if you have a clear direction, a Summary can be very effective. It can tell the reader in a few seconds: who you are, what you can do, and what role you fit.

### Where Does the Summary Go?

If you write a Summary, it goes at the very top of the resume, even above Education.

Why? Because the Summary's job is to position you. Before the Recruiter starts scanning, the Summary tells them: this person is a Front-End Engineer, a Data Scientist, or a Machine Learning Engineer. That positioning shapes how they interpret everything that follows.

### Multiple Directions = Multiple Summaries = Multiple Resumes

This brings us back to the earlier point: if you want to apply to multiple directions, you need multiple resumes, each with a different Summary.

Let me illustrate with examples. Imagine you're a CS new grad who has done some web development, some data analysis, and some machine learning projects. You want to apply to SDE, Data Engineer, and Machine Learning Engineer roles.

**Summary for a Software Engineer (SDE) role:**

> *Computer Science new grad with hands-on experience building full-stack web applications. Proficient in Python, JavaScript, React, and Node.js. Developed and deployed 3 production-ready projects with 500+ users.*

This Summary emphasizes full-stack ability, web stack, and production experience.

**Summary for a Data Engineer role:**

> *Computer Science new grad with strong foundation in data systems and ETL pipelines. Experienced with Python, SQL, Spark, and AWS. Built automated data pipelines processing 100K+ records daily in academic projects.*

This Summary emphasizes data systems, pipeline building, and large-scale data processing.

**Summary for a Machine Learning Engineer role:**

> *Computer Science new grad specializing in machine learning and deep learning. Proficient in Python, PyTorch, and TensorFlow. Implemented and trained CNN models achieving 94% accuracy on image classification tasks.*

This Summary emphasizes ML/DL ability, model training experience, and concrete metrics.

See the difference? Same person, three completely different positionings.

### More Summary Examples Across Directions

Here are more Summary examples across different role directions, to help you see how positioning shifts.

**AI Developer (application-focused):**

> *Computer Science new grad focused on building AI-powered applications. Experienced with Python, LangChain, and OpenAI APIs. Developed a RAG-based chatbot handling 1000+ queries in production environment.*

**AI Engineer (model and systems focused):**

> *Computer Science new grad with expertise in deploying ML models at scale. Proficient in Python, PyTorch, Docker, and Kubernetes. Built end-to-end ML pipelines from training to inference serving.*

**Data Analyst:**

> *Statistics and Computer Science new grad with strong analytical skills. Proficient in Python, SQL, Tableau, and Excel. Conducted data analysis projects uncovering insights that informed business decisions.*

**Data Scientist:**

> *Computer Science new grad with a focus on statistical modeling and machine learning. Experienced with Python, R, scikit-learn, and Pandas. Built predictive models with 85%+ accuracy on real-world datasets.*

**Cloud Engineer:**

> *Computer Science new grad with hands-on experience in cloud infrastructure. Proficient in AWS (EC2, S3, Lambda), Terraform, and Docker. Deployed and managed cloud-based applications with 99.9% uptime.*

**Solution Architect (less common for new grads, but some companies hire):**

> *Computer Science new grad with broad technical foundation across cloud, data, and application development. AWS Certified. Designed system architectures for 3 academic capstone projects involving microservices and distributed systems.*

**Financial Engineer / Quantitative Developer (fintech direction):**

> *Mathematics and Computer Science new grad with strong foundation in quantitative analysis. Proficient in Python, C++, and statistical modeling. Developed trading strategy backtesting framework processing 1M+ historical data points.*

You'll notice every Summary follows the same structure:

1. Who you are (academic background)
2. What your core skills are
3. What you've done (with concrete results)

But the skills and experience being emphasized are completely different.

## 7. Education

For new grads, Education is one of your most credible credentials. If you don't write a Summary, Education goes at the very top. If you do have a Summary, Education follows immediately after.

What goes in Education? School name, degree, major, graduation date (or expected graduation date). If your GPA stands out (say 3.5 or above), include it. If it isn't strong, leave it off. Nobody will ask why it's missing.

You can list Relevant Coursework, especially courses tied to your target role. But pick which courses to list based on the direction you're applying to.

Applying for Machine Learning Engineer? List:

> *Relevant Coursework: Machine Learning, Deep Learning, Statistical Learning, Linear Algebra, Probability and Statistics*

Applying for Software Engineer? List:

> *Relevant Coursework: Data Structures and Algorithms, Operating Systems, Database Systems, Software Engineering, Computer Networks*

Applying for Data Engineer? List:

> *Relevant Coursework: Database Systems, Distributed Systems, Data Mining, Cloud Computing, Big Data Analytics*

One more note: if you have notable accomplishments at school (winning a Hackathon, leading a coding club, serving as a course TA), you can include them in the Education section. They show you weren't just passively sitting through classes.

## 8. Skills

The Skills section needs to be precise and targeted.

The most common mistake here is listing every technology you've ever touched. Python, Java, C++, JavaScript, TypeScript, React, Vue, Angular, Node.js, Django, Flask, Spring, TensorFlow, PyTorch, AWS, GCP, Azure...

If you list 15 programming languages and 20 frameworks, the signal isn't "this person is impressive." The signal is "this person probably isn't strong at any of them."

Hiring Managers want a Specialist, not a Generalist.

The Skills section also needs to flex to the direction you're applying to. The same person can have completely different Skills sections for different roles.

**Skills for an SDE role:**

> *Languages: Python, JavaScript, TypeScript, Java*
> *Frameworks: React, Node.js, Express, Django*
> *Tools: Git, Docker, PostgreSQL, Redis*

**Skills for a Data Engineer role:**

> *Languages: Python, SQL, Scala*
> *Data Tools: Spark, Airflow, dbt, Kafka*
> *Cloud: AWS (S3, Redshift, Glue), Snowflake*

**Skills for a Machine Learning Engineer role:**

> *Languages: Python, C++*
> *ML Frameworks: PyTorch, TensorFlow, scikit-learn*
> *Tools: Docker, MLflow, Weights & Biases, Jupyter*

**Skills for a Cloud Engineer role:**

> *Cloud Platforms: AWS (EC2, S3, Lambda, RDS), GCP*
> *Infrastructure: Terraform, Docker, Kubernetes*
> *Languages: Python, Bash, YAML*

See the pattern? Each version lists only the skills most relevant to the target role, grouped logically.

A practical test: only list things you can confidently talk about in an interview. If the interviewer asks "what have you done with Kubernetes," can you answer? If not, don't list it.

## 9. Projects: The Heart of a New Grad Resume

This is the most important section of a new grad resume.

Without work experience, your projects are your experience. The Projects section proves you weren't just attending classes and doing homework. You were also writing code outside class, solving real problems.

### Personal Project vs Homework

There's a crucial distinction here: **a Personal Project and a Homework assignment are not the same thing.**

If everyone in your class wrote the same Blackjack CLI game, that isn't a Personal Project. That's Homework. Listing it not only doesn't help, it exposes a lack of independent initiative. It's a Red Flag.

What are employers looking for? People who, out of genuine curiosity, build things outside class and outside work. People who love the work go further and improve faster.

What kinds of projects are worth listing? A project that solves a real problem. A project that shows technical depth. A project that explores a meaningful technical question.

### How to Write Project Descriptions: Action + Technical Detail + Impact

A lot of new grad project descriptions look like this:

> *Built a To-Do List app using React and Node.js.*

What's wrong with that sentence? It only says what technology you used. It doesn't say what you did, what problem you solved, or what came out of it.

A good project description follows this formula: **Action + Technical Detail + Impact**.

**Action (what you did)**

Open with a concrete verb that describes what you did.

One important reminder here: **new grads should avoid certain "big words."**

What counts as a big word? Things like "Spearheaded," "Architected," "Pioneered," "Orchestrated." These words imply leadership roles or architect-level work. If you're a new grad doing a class project on your own, these words come off as inauthentic.

Which words work for new grads?

- Built
- Developed
- Implemented
- Designed
- Created
- Optimized
- Automated
- Integrated

These words are strong enough without sounding like you're inflating your role.

**Technical Detail**

Explain what technologies you used and how you put them together. But don't just stack technology names. Show that you actually used these technologies for something specific.

Bad version:

> *Used React, Node.js, MongoDB, Express.*

Good version:

> *Built responsive frontend with React and TypeScript, RESTful API with Node.js/Express, and MongoDB for data persistence.*

The second version makes it clear you understand the role each technology plays in the system.

**Impact**

This is the most overlooked part, and also the one that needs the most care.

One important principle for Impact numbers: **if you can't defend it under follow-up questions, don't write it.**

What does that mean? If you write "reduced page load time by 50%," the interviewer might ask:

- How did you measure it?
- What was the before number, what was the after number?
- What tool did you use to measure?
- What optimization caused the improvement?

If you can't answer, that number becomes a Red Flag and casts doubt on everything else on your resume.

So what do you do? Do your homework before writing Impact. Ask yourself:

- Is this number within a reasonable industry range?
- How did I measure it? What method, what tool?
- If pressed, can I explain it clearly?

You can even check your Impact numbers with an AI before putting them on the resume. Something like:

> "I built a web app and optimized image loading. Page load time went from 3 seconds to 1.5 seconds. Is that a reasonable number for the industry? I measured it with Chrome DevTools Lighthouse. Is that the right method?"

Work through the thought experiment first, then write it on the resume.

If you genuinely don't have reliable numbers, describe the problem the project solves or the potential application. Impact without numbers is still Impact.

> *A Chrome extension that helps fitness enthusiasts match Spotify songs by BPM for workout playlists, eliminating manual playlist curation.*

No numbers in that sentence, but it's clear the project solves a real problem.

### Project Description Examples Across Directions

Here are project description examples across different role directions, to help you see how to write Bullet Points for each one.

**SDE / Full-Stack direction:**

## 10. Work Experience (Internships)

If you have internship experience, this section is extremely valuable.

Even a single summer internship is proof that "someone paid you to do work." That's fundamentally different from a class project.

Internship entries follow the same pattern as projects, with a few extra notes.

### Establish Ownership

In your descriptions, show that this was "your" work, not just work "you participated in."

Bad version:

> *Participated in the team's data analysis project.*

Good version:

> *Owned the data cleaning and preprocessing module, processing 100K+ customer records daily.*

Words like "Owned," "Led," and "Responsible for" signal that you had Ownership, not that you were an intern just following instructions.

But once again: stay honest. If you only handled a small piece, don't write it as if you led the whole project.

### Quantify Impact (Carefully)

The benefit of an internship is that you may have real business data. The same principle still applies though: **if you can't defend it under follow-up questions, don't write it.**

Reasonable Impact descriptions look like:

> *Reduced report generation time from 2 hours to 15 minutes through automation.*
>
> *Improved model inference speed by 30% through code optimization and caching.*
>
> *Contributed to a feature that was shipped to 10K+ users.*

Notice these are all verifiable. You know it used to take 2 hours, you know it takes 15 minutes now, and you have a way to explain how you measured.

### On Data Confidentiality

A reminder here: always assume internal data is confidential. Before putting specific Metrics on your resume, get permission, or obscure them with percentages or multipliers rather than raw numbers.

For example, you can't say "the company's DAU grew from 1 million to 1.5 million," but you can say "DAU increased by 50%."

## 11. Common Mistakes on New Grad Resumes

Let me run through the new grad resume mistakes I've seen most often.

### Mistake 1: Listing Too Many Technologies

You list 15 programming languages and 20 frameworks. The signal that sends is you probably aren't strong at any of them. Focus on the technologies you're actually good at. For the direction you're applying to, list the 3 to 5 most relevant core skills.

### Mistake 2: Treating Class Assignments as Personal Projects

If everyone in your class built the same project, that's Homework, not a Personal Project. You need at least one or two projects you genuinely built independently, driven by personal interest or a real problem.

### Mistake 3: Only Listing Technologies, Not What You Did

> *Built an app using React, Node.js, MongoDB.*

This sentence doesn't tell the reader what you did. What problem did you solve? What features did it have? What came out of it?

### Mistake 4: Inflated Numbers You Can't Back Up

> *Improved performance by 500%.*

If the interviewer asks how you measured it, what the before and after numbers were, and what you did to cause the improvement, can you answer? If not, don't write it.

### Mistake 5: Using "Big Words" New Grads Shouldn't Use

"Spearheaded," "Architected," "Pioneered." These words imply leadership roles or architect-level work. If you're a student who finished a course project on your own, these words feel inauthentic. "Built," "Developed," and "Implemented" are plenty.

### Mistake 6: GitHub Link Pointing to an Empty Repository

You include a GitHub link, the reader clicks through, and it's empty or the last commit was two years ago. Either clean it up first, or leave it off.

### Mistake 7: Resume Longer Than One Page

If you're a new grad, you don't need a two-page resume. If you feel you can't fit everything on one page, your resume isn't tight enough yet. Recruiters spend roughly six seconds per resume on average. If they have to flip the page, you're basically out.

### Mistake 8: One Resume for Every Role

You send the same resume for SDE, Data Engineer, and ML Engineer, and none of them look focused. Preparing different versions for different directions noticeably lifts your interview conversion rate.

## 12. Strategy Differences Between the Two Scenarios

Back to the two scenarios from the start. As a new grad facing large company campus recruiting versus direct applications to small and mid-size companies, how does the strategy differ?

### Large Company Campus Recruiting

Large company campus recruiting is bulk filtering. Your resume sits in a pile with thousands of others and gets a quick scan by a Recruiter.

In this scenario, you need to make sure you pass the standardized filter. School, GPA, keyword match. These hard metrics matter. Make sure your resume lets the Recruiter check the boxes quickly. You need to show that you "meet the bar." Large company campus recruiting cares whether you're "qualified," not how unique you are. Make sure the basics are there: relevant major, relevant skills, relevant projects. Tech stack alignment also matters. If the JD asks for Java and Spring, make sure your Skills and Projects show them.

### Direct Applications to Small and Mid-size Companies

Small and mid-size companies don't have a dedicated campus recruiting pipeline. Your resume sits in the same pool as candidates with experience.

In this scenario, you have to stand out. You're competing with people who have worked 2 to 3 years. "Meeting the bar" isn't enough. You need the Hiring Manager to feel "this person is worth a shot." You need to show potential. You don't have work experience, but you can show learning ability, initiative, and problem solving. Your Personal Projects matter especially here. You need to tell a story. Why, despite not having experience, are you worth hiring? Your projects, your technical depth, your approach to problems. These can build a story that convinces someone.

What the two scenarios share: clarity, Impact, and scannability are still the core. Only the emphasis shifts.

## 13. Core Takeaways

Let me sum up the core points of this post.

First, you probably need more than one resume. For each direction, prepare a different version with a different Summary, different Skills emphasis, and different project ordering.

Second, if you write a Summary, put it at the very top. Its job is to position who you are and what you can do.

Third, Projects are the most important section of a new grad resume. They prove you weren't just attending classes, you were solving real problems outside of them too.

Fourth, project descriptions should follow the Action + Technical Detail + Impact formula. Use real, verifiable verbs and numbers. Skip the big words. Skip the inflated numbers you can't defend.

Fifth, on Impact numbers: if you can't defend it under follow-up questions, don't write it. Before writing it, work out how you measured and how you'd explain it.

Sixth, Skills should be precise and tuned to the direction you're applying to. Don't list every technology you've ever touched.

## 14. Mentor's Note

I've met plenty of new grads whose technical ability is actually strong, but whose resume looks like they threw it together in 15 minutes.

They list a pile of technology names but don't show what they've done. They list every class project but don't have a single piece they truly built on their own. Their Impact numbers look pretty but don't hold up under questioning.

This isn't an ability problem. It's an expression problem.

Your resume is answering one question: why should we interview this person?

For a new grad, the answer isn't "because I've studied a lot of technologies." The answer is "because I can get things done."

How do you prove you can get things done? Prove it with things you've done.

That's why Personal Projects matter so much. They are the only place you can show "I didn't just study, I built."

If you feel your current projects aren't enough to carry a strong resume, that's a signal: go Build More.

Find a problem you genuinely care about and use what you've learned to solve it. Finish it, deploy it, get real people using it. The process itself is the best kind of learning, and you'll end up with a real outcome you can put on the resume.

Before writing Impact numbers, work out how you measured and how you'd explain it. You can talk it through with an AI, you can write code to run experiments, you can look up industry benchmarks. Do the homework up front so every word on your resume can hold up under questioning.

People who love the work go further and improve faster. Let your resume show that.
