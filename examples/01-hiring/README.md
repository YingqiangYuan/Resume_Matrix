## 3. Core Concepts

### The Central Question, Who Is Your Resume For?

Before you write a single line of experience, ask yourself:

- Whose hands will this resume pass through?
- Who screens? Who decides?
- What are they looking for?

Different hiring scenarios mean different readers. Once you grasp this, your entire approach to writing a resume shifts.

### Scenario One, Large Company Campus Recruiting

**Typical companies**: FAANG, Big 4 accounting firms, large banks, well-known tech companies

**Who's hiring**: The University Recruiting team, the HR group that specializes in campus hires.

**How the process works**:

1. The company finalizes next year's headcount (hiring slots)
2. The University Recruiting team picks target schools (usually tiered)
3. They attend Career Fairs and host Info Sessions on campus
4. They collect resumes through school platforms (like Handshake) or the company website
5. A large volume of resumes flows into the system for standardized screening (ATS plus human review)
6. Candidates who pass move into Online Assessment, Phone Screen, and On-site interviews
7. Offers go out in batches, with a unified start date

**What this model looks like**:

- Bulk hiring, assembly-line style, standardized process
- Hard metrics carry heavy weight. School reputation, GPA, major, keyword match
- No specific Hiring Manager is tracking you from the start. You get screened first and assigned to a team after the offer

**What this means for your resume**: Your first hurdle is passing standardized screening. The resume needs to "look qualified" so the ATS and Recruiter can check the boxes quickly.

### Scenario Two, Small and Mid-Size Company Hiring (New Grads Applying Directly)

**Typical companies**: Startups, mid-size tech companies, places without a dedicated campus recruiting pipeline

**Who's hiring**: A Hiring Manager has a need and either reads resumes themselves or has a Recruiter help screen.

**How the process works**:

1. A Hiring Manager is short-staffed (team needs more people, new project starting)
2. They write a Job Description and post it on LinkedIn, the company website, or Indeed
3. Resumes trickle in. Could be dozens, could be hundreds
4. The Recruiter or Hiring Manager screens them personally
5. Phone interview, technical interview, On-site, Offer

**What this model looks like**:

- **New grads compete in the same pool as experienced candidates**
- There's no "campus-only lane." Your resume sits next to people with 2 to 3 years of experience
- The process is more flexible, and the Hiring Manager has more influence

**What this means for your resume**: You need to stand out. With no work experience, you have to lean on projects, potential, and learning ability to make the Hiring Manager think, "This person is worth a conversation."

**Important note**: A lot of new grads job-hunting in North America are actually applying to roles like this. Don't assume big-company campus recruiting is the only path. But it also means your resume has to be more competitive.

### Scenario Three, Experienced Hire (Career Switchers)

**Typical situation**: People with 1 to 5 years of experience changing jobs

**Who's hiring**: The Hiring Manager has a specific need and works with an Internal Recruiter or Agency Recruiter.

**How the process works**:

1. The Hiring Manager clarifies exactly what kind of person they need (skills, experience, level)
2. The Recruiter posts the position and actively sources candidates
3. Resumes flood in (hot roles can pull 1,000+)
4. The Recruiter narrows it down to 20 to 30 and hands them to the Hiring Manager
5. The Hiring Manager picks who moves into the interview pipeline
6. Interviews, Offer

**What this model looks like**:

- The Hiring Manager has a concrete problem to solve (missing a skill, filling a seat)
- Your resume has to show that you can solve their problem
- Relevant experience and concrete results matter a lot

**What this means for your resume**: Show that you have relevant experience and can hit the ground running. Not "I'm impressive," but "I can help you solve problem X."

## 4. Key Roles

### Hiring Manager

- The person who actually needs to make the hire
- The final call on whether you get the offer
- What they care about. Can this person solve my problem? Will they fit on the team?

### Recruiter

- The person who screens resumes for the Hiring Manager
- The gatekeeper. Your resume has to clear them first
- Two flavors:
  - **Internal Recruiter**: In-house HR who knows the company culture and needs
  - **Agency Recruiter**: External headhunter, paid on successful placement

### ATS (Applicant Tracking System)

- An automated resume management system
- Helps with screening, ranking, and tracking candidates
- We'll go deep on how the ATS works in 02-ats

---

## 5. Core Insights

**Insight one. Different scenarios, different readers to win over**

- Large company campus recruiting. Win over the ATS and Recruiter first (standardized screening)
- Small and mid-size companies. Win over the Hiring Manager directly (they may read your resume personally)
- Experienced hire. Show that you can solve the Hiring Manager's specific problem

**Insight two. New grads at small and mid-size companies are running the experienced-hire gauntlet**

- You're competing with people who have experience
- The resume needs to highlight projects and potential to make up for the experience gap

**Insight three. The point of a resume is to land an interview**

- It's not about proving you're impressive
- It's about making the reader think, "Worth a conversation"

## 6. Mentor's Note

**Why this post matters**

I've seen too many job seekers treat resume writing as a purely mechanical task (fill in the template, list responsibilities, then pray someone notices). But a resume is communication. Effective communication starts with knowing who you're talking to.

Once you understand that a startup Hiring Manager might read every single resume personally, while a large company's hiring machine uses automated systems to process tens of thousands, your strategy shifts. The words you pick shift. The order of your bullet points shifts.

**A few key things to internalize**:

- There's no such thing as "one good resume that works everywhere." Only resumes that win over specific readers in specific scenarios.
- Most new grads underestimate how fierce the competition is when they apply outside the campus recruiting system, going head to head with experienced candidates.
- The hiring process isn't something that "happens to you." It's a system you can understand and respond to strategically.
