# How to Write a Resume with 1 to 5 Years of Experience, from What I Did to What I Accomplished

> This is the fourth post in the examples series. Earlier posts covered new-grad resumes. This one is for working professionals with experience, especially those in the 1 to 5 year range.

## 1. Course Introduction

If you already have some work experience and are thinking about switching jobs or changing directions, this post is for you.

Once you have work experience, the rules of the resume game change fundamentally.

A new grad's challenge is "I have no experience, how do I prove I can do the job?" Your challenge is different. You have experience, you've done real work. The question becomes: **how do you show what you actually accomplished?**

Plenty of experienced people still write resumes that read like a list of Job Duties. "Responsible for development and maintenance of the XX system." "Participated in the design and implementation of the XX project." Lines like that tell the reader what tasks you were assigned, but they don't say anything about how well you did them or what results came out of the work.

This post is about solving that problem.

As with the rest of the series, this tutorial is written in Chinese but teaches you how to write an **English resume for the North American job market**. Every example bullet, Summary, and resume excerpt will be in English.

---

## 2. Learning Objectives

Let's recap what we covered in 01-hiring. Experienced job seekers go through the lateral hiring process.

The Hiring Manager has a specific need (a missing skill, an open seat, a problem that needs solving). The Recruiter posts the role, does active sourcing, and resumes flood in. A hot role can easily attract 1,000+ applications. The Recruiter filters down to 20 to 30 resumes for the Hiring Manager, and the Hiring Manager picks who moves into the interview loop.

In that flow, what does the Hiring Manager actually want to see?

**That you can solve their problem.**

Not "you're impressive," not "you've studied a lot of technologies," but "you can help me solve problem X."

Your resume needs to show that you have relevant experience, that you can hit the ground running, and that you've done similar work before and succeeded at it.

By the end of this post, you'll know what an experienced person's resume structure should look like, how to write Work Experience that shows Impact, how to do Tailoring for different roles, and what the most common mistakes are.

## 3. Experienced Resumes vs New Grad Resumes, What Changed?

Before we dig into how to write one, let's get clear on what shifts once you have experience.

### Work Experience becomes the core

For new grads, Projects is the core of the resume. Without work experience, projects are the only place that proves capability.

For experienced people, **Work Experience becomes the core**. Your job history itself is the proof. You no longer need Side Projects to fill the page. Your full-time work is the most persuasive content you have.

This doesn't mean Projects are completely irrelevant. If you have a standout Side Project, an Open Source Contribution, or a personal project closely tied to what you're applying for, you can still list it. But it's no longer required.

### Education carries less weight

For new grads, Education sits at the top of the resume because it's your strongest credential.

For someone with 3 to 4 years of experience, **Education should move to the bottom**. Your work history speaks louder than your degree. The Hiring Manager cares much more about what you did at Google than which school you graduated from.

If you went to a top-tier school like MIT or Stanford, you can consider keeping Education a bit higher. Even then, Work Experience outweighs Education by a wide margin.

### You can use stronger verbs now

In 03-new-grad, I told you to avoid heavyweight verbs like "Spearheaded" and "Architected," because new grads doing solo class projects sound implausible when they use that language.

If you genuinely have 2 to 3 years of experience, have actually led a team, and have actually designed architecture, you can absolutely use those words.

The key: **the words have to match what you actually did**.

If you're an IC (Individual Contributor) mostly executing tasks others designed, don't write "Led a team of 5 engineers." But if you really did drive a project, mentor people, and make technical decisions, show it clearly.

### Quantified Impact matters more

New grad project Impact is sometimes hard to quantify. Many projects are personal or academic, with no real business data behind them.

With work experience, you should have real business data.

> *Reduced API response time from 500ms to 200ms.*
> *Refactored system cut bug reports by 40%.*
> *Feature launched with 25% DAU growth.*

Those numbers are your strongest weapon. Section 7.2 below ("How to find your Impact numbers") goes deeper on how to collect them, and later [09-write-bullets](../09-write-bullets/README.md) covers how to write them into a bullet that holds up under questioning.

## 4. Structure of an Experienced Resume

Let's pin down which sections an experienced resume should include and in what order.

The recommended order:

1. Contact Information
2. Summary (positions who you are and what you can do)
3. Skills (placed early so readers can quickly confirm fit)
4. Work Experience (the core section)
5. Projects (optional, only if you have something especially relevant)
6. Education (at the bottom)

The biggest difference from a new grad resume: Work Experience is now the most important block, and Education drops to the end.

Let's go through each one.

---

## 5. Summary, Tell the Reader Who You Are

For experienced candidates, a Summary is essentially required.

Why? Because you have a professional identity now. You're no longer a generalist new grad who "tried a bit of everything." You're someone with a defined specialty. The Summary's job is to announce, right at the top of the resume, who this person is. A Backend Engineer, a Data Engineer, an ML Engineer, with X years of experience, strong in Y and Z.

A good Summary has three elements:

1. Your professional identity and years of experience
2. Your core skills or specialty
3. Your most notable achievement (optional, but a plus)

### Summary examples for different roles

**Senior Software Engineer (backend focus):**

> *Backend Engineer with 4 years of experience building scalable distributed systems. Proficient in Java, Go, and AWS. Led the migration of a monolithic application to microservices, reducing deployment time by 60% and improving system reliability to 99.9% uptime.*

This Summary tells the reader: 4 years of experience, backend focus, distributed systems, with concrete achievement numbers.

**Senior Software Engineer (full-stack focus):**

> *Full-Stack Engineer with 3 years of experience shipping user-facing products. Proficient in React, Node.js, and PostgreSQL. Built and launched 5 features that collectively serve 500K+ monthly active users.*

**Data Engineer:**

> *Data Engineer with 4 years of experience building and optimizing data pipelines at scale. Expert in Python, Spark, and Airflow. Architected a real-time data pipeline processing 10M+ events daily with 99.9% reliability.*

**Machine Learning Engineer:**

> *Machine Learning Engineer with 3 years of experience deploying ML models in production. Proficient in Python, PyTorch, and MLOps tools. Built recommendation systems serving 1M+ daily predictions with <100ms latency.*

**Data Scientist:**

> *Data Scientist with 4 years of experience in predictive modeling and experimentation. Expert in Python, SQL, and statistical analysis. Led A/B testing initiatives that drove $2M+ in incremental annual revenue.*

**Cloud Engineer / DevOps Engineer:**

> *Cloud Engineer with 3 years of experience managing production infrastructure on AWS. Expert in Terraform, Kubernetes, and CI/CD pipelines. Reduced infrastructure costs by 30% while improving deployment frequency by 5x.*

**AI Engineer:**

> *AI Engineer with 3 years of experience building LLM-powered applications. Proficient in Python, LangChain, and vector databases. Developed a RAG system that reduced customer support ticket volume by 35%.*

**Solutions Architect:**

> *Solutions Architect with 5 years of experience designing enterprise cloud architectures. AWS Certified Solutions Architect Professional. Led cloud migration projects totaling $10M+ in infrastructure, achieving 40% cost reduction.*

**Financial Engineer / Quant Developer:**

> *Quantitative Developer with 4 years of experience in trading systems and risk analytics. Proficient in Python, C++, and SQL. Built low-latency trading infrastructure processing 50K+ orders per second.*

You'll notice every Summary follows the same structure but emphasizes completely different content. That's the whole point of Tailoring.

### Summary goes at the top

Just like for new grads, if you write a Summary it goes at the very top of the resume, above Skills and Work Experience.

The Summary's role is positioning. Before the Recruiter starts scanning your resume, the Summary already tells them what direction you're in, what level you're at, and what your core strengths are. That framing colors how they interpret everything that follows.

## 6. Skills, Precise and Targeted

The principle here is the same as for new grads: **be precise, be targeted, only list things you can confidently talk about**.

Experienced candidates have one extra consideration: you should be able to back up these skills with your work history.

If you list Kubernetes under Skills, the reader expects to see Kubernetes actually used somewhere in your Work Experience. If you only followed a Tutorial once and never used it on a real job, leave it off.

### Skills examples by direction

**Backend Engineer:**

> *Languages: Java, Go, Python*
> *Frameworks: Spring Boot, gRPC, GraphQL*
> *Infrastructure: AWS (EC2, RDS, SQS), Docker, Kubernetes*
> *Databases: PostgreSQL, Redis, DynamoDB*

**Full-Stack Engineer:**

> *Frontend: React, TypeScript, Next.js*
> *Backend: Node.js, Python, Django*
> *Databases: PostgreSQL, MongoDB, Redis*
> *Cloud: AWS, Vercel, Docker*

**Data Engineer:**

> *Languages: Python, SQL, Scala*
> *Data Tools: Spark, Airflow, dbt, Kafka*
> *Cloud: AWS (S3, Redshift, Glue, EMR), Snowflake*
> *Databases: PostgreSQL, BigQuery, Delta Lake*

**Machine Learning Engineer:**

> *Languages: Python, C++*
> *ML Frameworks: PyTorch, TensorFlow, scikit-learn*
> *MLOps: MLflow, Kubeflow, Weights & Biases*
> *Infrastructure: Docker, Kubernetes, AWS SageMaker*

**Cloud Engineer / DevOps:**

> *Cloud Platforms: AWS (certified), GCP*
> *Infrastructure as Code: Terraform, CloudFormation, Pulumi*
> *Orchestration: Kubernetes, ECS, Docker*
> *CI/CD: GitHub Actions, Jenkins, ArgoCD*

Skills belongs between Summary and Work Experience. Its job is to let readers confirm quickly whether your stack matches their need. If it matches, they'll keep going into the Work Experience details.

## 7. Work Experience, the Core of the Resume

This is the single most important block on an experienced resume.

Your work history is your strongest proof. The question is: how do you write it so readers can see your value?

### The core formula: Action + Technical Detail + Measurable Impact

Just like new grad project descriptions, Work Experience bullets follow this formula:

**Action (what you did) + Technical Detail (the technical specifics) + Measurable Impact (the quantifiable result)**

Experienced candidates can push it further.

**Action: establish Ownership**

As an experienced person, you should be able to demonstrate a higher level of Ownership.

Weak:

> *Worked on the payment system.*

Strong:

> *Led the redesign of the payment processing system, coordinating with 3 cross-functional teams.*

"Led," "Owned," "Drove," "Architected." These words signal you weren't just executing, you were the decision-maker, the driver.

Same principle applies: **stay honest**. If you were one of several engineers on a team without leading anything, use "Contributed to" or "Collaborated on." Inflating your role only blows up in the interview.

**Technical Detail: show depth**

Technical detail should show professional depth, not stack a pile of buzzwords.

Weak:

> *Used Java, Spring Boot, AWS, Kubernetes, Docker, Redis.*

Strong:

> *Built a microservices architecture using Spring Boot and gRPC, deployed on Kubernetes with automated scaling based on traffic patterns.*

The second version makes it clear you understand how these technologies fit together, not that you can list them in a row.

**Measurable Impact: let numbers do the talking**

This is the experienced candidate's biggest advantage. You have real business data.

Weak:

> *Improved system performance significantly.*

Strong:

> *Reduced API latency from 500ms to 150ms (70% improvement), resulting in 15% increase in user engagement.*

Concrete numbers carry far more weight than a vague "significant improvement."

### How to find your Impact numbers

A lot of people say "I don't know what Impact my work had." The Impact is there, you just haven't gone looking for it or written it down.

Here are a few ways to dig up your Impact numbers.

**Internal sources:**

Your day job actually has plenty of data lying around. Talk to your Manager and ask what business effect your project had. Talk to the Product Manager (they usually have post-launch numbers). Look at your team's Analytics Dashboard (DAU, conversion rate, error rate, those kinds of metrics). Revisit All-Hands Meetings or Quarterly Reviews where individual project Impact often gets called out. Check the A/B test results for experiments you were part of.

**External sources:**

Some data is available publicly. Company Press Releases or Blog posts. Public-facing product data (App Store downloads, user reviews, etc.). Public companies' Quarterly Earnings Calls and 10-K Filings. Your coworkers' LinkedIn Profiles, where you can see how they describe the Impact of similar projects.

**On data confidentiality**

Important reminder here: always assume internal data is confidential.

Before putting concrete numbers on a resume, it's best to ask your Manager for permission. If that's not an option, blur the numbers into percentages or multiples rather than raw figures.

For example:

- Don't say "Increased DAU from 1M to 1.5M"
- Do say "Increased DAU by 50%"

- Don't say "Reduced infrastructure costs from $100K/month to $70K/month"
- Do say "Reduced infrastructure costs by 30%"

**What if you really don't have numbers**

Sometimes Impact is genuinely hard to quantify. Maybe you do infrastructure work, or the project hasn't launched yet.

In that case, describe the scale and complexity of the project, or describe the importance of the problem you solved.

> *Architected a new authentication system supporting 10M+ user accounts and 50+ microservices.*

> *Built the core data pipeline powering the company's primary analytics platform, processing 5TB of data daily.*

These descriptions don't carry "improved by X%" outcome numbers, but they show the scale of the project and what you contributed.

## 9. Career Trajectory, How to Show Promotions and Growth

If you've been promoted at the same company, or your scope clearly expanded, that's a great opportunity to put on display.

### Promotion within the same company

If you got promoted from Junior to Senior at the same company, you can write it this way:

**Company Name | San Francisco, CA**

**Senior Software Engineer | Jan 2023 to Present**
> *Promoted to Senior Engineer after demonstrating technical leadership and impact.*
>
> *Led the redesign of the core API layer, improving throughput by 3x and reducing error rate by 80%.*
>
> *Mentored 3 junior engineers, helping 2 of them get promoted within 18 months.*

**Software Engineer | Jun 2021 to Dec 2022**
> *Built and shipped the user notification system, serving 1M+ daily notifications with 99.9% delivery rate.*
>
> *Developed automated testing infrastructure, increasing code coverage from 45% to 85%.*

This layout makes your career trajectory visible. You weren't sitting at the same level doing the same thing. You were growing and taking on more responsibility.

### Showing scope expansion

Even without a formal promotion, if your scope grew, you can show that:

> *Started as the sole engineer on a 3-person team; grew the team to 8 engineers while maintaining technical leadership of the core platform.*

> *Expanded scope from individual contributor to technical lead, owning architecture decisions for a system serving 10M+ users.*

## 10. Tailoring, Adjusting the Resume for Different Roles

Just like new grads, experienced people need different resume versions for different roles.

Experienced Tailoring can go deeper, because you have more raw material to choose from and reorganize.

### Same experience, different emphasis

Say at one company you did backend development, data processing, and some ML-related work. For different roles, you can emphasize different Bullet Points.

**Applying for Backend Engineer roles, emphasize:**

> *Led the migration of a monolithic application to microservices, reducing deployment time by 60%.*
>
> *Designed and implemented high-throughput API endpoints handling 10K+ requests per second.*
>
> *Optimized database queries, reducing average response time by 70%.*

**Applying for Data Engineer roles, emphasize:**

> *Built real-time data pipelines using Kafka and Spark, processing 5M+ events daily.*
>
> *Designed data models and ETL workflows for analytics platform used by 50+ internal users.*
>
> *Implemented data quality monitoring, reducing data incidents by 60%.*

**Applying for ML Engineer roles, emphasize:**

> *Built and deployed ML models for user behavior prediction, serving 1M+ daily inferences.*
>
> *Developed feature engineering pipelines processing large-scale user data.*
>
> *Implemented A/B testing framework for model evaluation and iteration.*

One experience block, three completely different angles of emphasis.

### Summary and Skills shift too

Naturally, the Summary and Skills move along with it. For a Backend role, use a Backend-flavored Summary and highlight your backend stack. For a Data Engineer role, swap to a Data-flavored Summary and highlight data tools.

This is exactly why I say you may need more than one resume. One version per direction, each one optimized for that direction.

## 11. Common Mistakes

Let me walk through the most common mistakes on experienced resumes.

### Mistake 1: writing Job Duties instead of Impact

> *Responsible for maintaining the payment system.*
>
> *Participated in code reviews and team meetings.*
>
> *Worked on various bug fixes and feature requests.*

These lines tell the reader what tasks you were given, but not how well you did them. Everyone in that role does these things. The real question is: once you did the work, what happened?

### Mistake 2: not quantifying Impact

> *Improved system performance significantly.*
>
> *Reduced bugs and improved code quality.*
>
> *Increased user engagement.*

"Significantly improved," "raised quality," "boosted engagement." Vague phrasing like this isn't persuasive. Use real numbers. By how much? From what to what? How was it measured?

### Mistake 3: stacking technologies

> *Proficient in Java, Python, Go, JavaScript, TypeScript, C++, Ruby, Scala, Kotlin, Swift, Rust, and 15 other programming languages.*

If you list this many languages, the reader will doubt whether you're truly fluent in any of them. Focus on what you actually own and what's relevant to the target role.

### Mistake 4: keeping Education at the top

If you have 3 to 4 years of work experience and Education is still at the top, the signal is: your work history isn't strong enough to carry the resume, so you're leaning on your degree.

Move Education to the bottom. Your work history is the lead.

### Mistake 5: resume longer than one page

Unless you have 10+ years of experience, one page is enough.

Many people feel they've done so much that one page can't hold it. Recruiters spend about 6 seconds per resume. Your goal isn't to list everything you've ever done. It's to list the most relevant, highest-Impact things you've done.

Tighten the content. Every Bullet Point should answer "why should we interview this person?" If a bullet can't do that, cut it.

### Mistake 6: one resume for all roles

You use one resume to apply for Backend, Data, and ML roles, and none of them feel focused.

Invest the time in different versions. Tailoring meaningfully lifts your interview conversion rate.

## 12. Core Insights

Let me pull together the key takeaways from this post.

First, once you have experience, Work Experience becomes the core of the resume. You no longer need Projects to prove yourself. Your job history is the proof.

Second, Education carries less weight and should move to the bottom. Your work history says more than your degree.

Third, write Impact, not Job Duties. Don't describe your responsibilities. Describe what happened after you did the work.

Fourth, let numbers do the talking. You have real business data, use it. "Cut latency by 70%" lands much harder than "significantly improved performance."

Fifth, actively collect your Impact numbers. Talk to your Manager and PM, watch the Dashboard, write down outcomes from projects you're part of. Do this work before you actually need to write the resume.

Sixth, prepare different versions for different roles. The same experience can be told from different angles. Tailoring matters.

## 13. A Note from Your Mentor

I've met plenty of experienced engineers whose actual work was valuable, but whose resumes read as flat and unremarkable.

What's the problem? They don't know how to surface their Impact.

They write Job Duties. Things like "responsible for development of the XX system," "participated in the XX project." Those lines tell readers what they were doing, not how well they did it.

The more common problem: they never recorded their Impact in the first place. The project shipped, they moved on to the next one. Years later when they sit down to write a resume, the concrete numbers are already gone from memory.

This is the single thing I most want to emphasize: **start recording your Impact today**.

Every time a project ships, record the outcome numbers. Every time you do an optimization, record the before and after. Every All-Hands that mentions a project you worked on, take a screenshot.

These records become your most valuable raw material when it's time to write the resume.

One more thing. Don't underestimate Tailoring. Yes, preparing multiple versions is a hassle. But think about it. You may need to apply to dozens of roles to land a few interviews. If Tailoring lifts your interview rate from 5% to 15%, that's a 3x difference. A few hours of preparing different versions is absolutely worth it.

Your resume is answering one question: why should we interview this person?

For experienced people, the answer is: because I've done something similar before, and I succeeded at it. Let your resume show that.
